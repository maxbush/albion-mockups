# US university admissions

An American admissions office reads a good deal more than grades. ALBION, an Oxford-based consultancy, guides candidates to Ivy League and other American universities, from choosing where to apply and sequencing the submissions to the personal essays each university asks for. Our US clients are families comparing the US with the UK, and adults looking at graduate and business programmes across the Atlantic.

## What a US file contains

A UCAS application is built around one subject: grades, a personal statement written for that subject, a reference. In the US, test scores and school grades sit beside essays, recommendations, what the student has done outside lessons, and a reasoned case for the particular programme. Each university may set its own essay prompts, so a single all-purpose draft rarely works.

Money enters the picture earlier too. Tuition and any financial aid depend on the individual university, which is why we raise the subject while the list is still being drawn up, not after the first replies arrive.

## How we work

An adviser reviews the candidate's academic record, subjects, test results, activities and intended direction, then proposes a list that mixes ambitious options with realistic ones. Each university is judged on where it leads, not on the name alone. The same adviser plans the order of applications and tracks each university's dates. Together with the student, the adviser works on the essays through several rounds of revision. Recommendation letters and portfolios are covered where a programme accepts them.

## Tests

Preparation runs through ALBION's own tutors, and the adviser follows progress, so that mock results feed back into the strategy. SAT is typically used for undergraduate admission, GRE for master's and doctoral programmes among others, and GMAT for business schools.

Test policies differ between universities and can change from one year to the next. We confirm the rules for your year of entry on each university's own site before any sitting is booked.

## What to expect, and what not to

No adviser can promise a place at an Ivy League university or at one of comparable selectivity, and we do not. The aim is a file strong enough to leave the candidate with several realistic outcomes. We quote fees after the first consultation; they depend on the level of study, the number of universities and the preparation required.

## From practice: a transfer to a university near Wall Street

A student at a Californian university, whose grades rose every term, wanted to become a trader and transfer to New York. His average was not enough for the university he had hoped for, so we added an option he had not heard of: a city college close to Wall Street with strong links to finance. His first application to the city system went unanswered: in September it said it could not reach a decision and invited him to apply again from 16 October. We applied on the first day, then spent months pushing through the transcript check and the documents for his visa form. He was accepted, lives in New York and says he is happier than he would have been at the first-choice university. Not everything depends on an adviser: universities can be slow, so it helps to have someone who sees it through.

## From practice: a conditional offer with no fallback

A pupil who at 13 was called a future professor lost interest in study by A-level, and people worried about him. He wanted the US, like his elder brother. We applied to several universities and bet on a campus of a state university system: not the strongest, but one standard of quality with the flagship. The offer was conditional: maths no lower than B, while C or even D was expected. The US has no insurance choice as UCAS does: by August you must accept one offer, and if the condition is not met only an expensive last-chance private university remains. We kept in touch with the university and prepared a plan B in advance. The results came, he met the condition, and when something went wrong on the maths and English placement tests we helped him through an extra module. He now earns B, A- and A, improving every term.

## From practice: weak grades are not the end of the road

A graduate of a British school with an academically weak profile wanted a US university, knowing his A-level results would be weak. Among several offers we chose a state university, to start from a base and think about a transfer later. In the essay we told openly how he had failed GCSE and what he had learned, and the university replied with a warm letter. The offer came before the A-level results. There he chose event management and found his place. His first attempt to transfer to a strong hospitality university after the first year ended in refusal: his average was very low. We applied again after the second year, at the end of the window, replacing the academic gap with personal qualities: a CV with event organisation and work with children, and an essay in the American style, which does not prove preparation but captures the reader. He was accepted, and the university wrote that it was impressed by his letter. He had to make up some courses and study took a year longer, but he is now finishing the programme and looking for work.

## Questions and answers

### Are A-Levels enough for a US application?

They form part of it. Admissions offices will also want essays, recommendations and often test results, so it helps to start early enough to build the non-academic side of the profile.

### Should I sit a test if a university does not require one?

It depends on the candidate and the programme. The adviser and tutor judge it after a diagnostic, before a date is booked.

### Do you advise on financial aid?

We raise it when the list of universities is drawn up, because policies vary. What an international applicant can access depends on the university concerned, and we establish that before anything is submitted. We cannot promise funding.

### I work full time. Can I prepare for the GMAT or GRE?

Yes. ALBION's tutors work across time zones, and lessons are scheduled around your working week. The application plan is adjusted as your scores develop.
