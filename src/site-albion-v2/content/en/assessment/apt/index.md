# APT (Academic Profiling Test): what it measures and how to read the scores

Parents usually meet the APT as a number: a standard age score showing how a child compares with others of the same age. The Academic Profiling Test is an adaptive online assessment for ages 6 to 16, and its developer, aptonline.co.uk, says it can be taken at home. Here is what sits behind the number, and what it cannot tell you.

## What the scores mean

The main figure is the Standard Age Score. It places a child on a scale of 50 to 150 against a representative sample of candidates who took the same test, with 100 as the average. Most children land between 90 and 110; above 110 is statistically stronger than the age group, below 90 weaker.

The report adds a stanine (a 1 to 9 band), an overall score averaging the four subjects, and an indicative equivalent on the CAT and CEM scales. According to the test's own site, a CAT equivalent of 108–112 is typical of a UK independent school. Treat it as an approximate conversion, not a CAT result. The most useful part is rarely the headline: each subject is broken into sub-skills, which shows where effort should go.

## The four sections

English covers grammar, comprehension, spelling and punctuation. Maths follows the UK National Curriculum up to Year 11 (GCSE): number, algebra, geometry, statistics and probability.

Verbal reasoning uses words: codes, patterns, written instructions. Non-verbal reasoning uses shapes and images, so it leans less on the child's English.

Questions are adaptive: difficulty moves with each answer, so the result shows where a child sits among peers, not how many items were solved.

## Taking it

You need a computer or laptop, a steady connection, a quiet room and a parent or guardian nearby to supervise. Timing depends on age: about 75 minutes for ages 6–9, around 90 for 10–13, around two hours for 14–16. Modules run to about 20 minutes, with breaks. The site says reports arrive within 24 hours.

## What the APT is not

Not an entrance exam. Schools set their own requirements (UKiset, ISEB Pre-Test, CAT4 and others) and decide on the offer. The APT site presents the test as familiarisation for that style of paper and as a way to show performance to future schools. Whether a given school will take notice of a result is for that school to say, so check first. Our page on [entrance exams](/private-schools/entrance-exams/) covers who asks for what.

It is also a snapshot of one or two hours. Writing at length, interview manner and depth in a single subject need past work and a conversation with a teacher.

## Questions and answers

### Does my child need to prepare for the APT?

The test's site says no specific preparation is needed: it measures general skills rather than learned knowledge, and short-term preparation will not move the result much. A few CAT-style sample questions can help a child feel at ease.

### Is the APT a substitute for UKiset or the ISEB Pre-Test?

No. Schools ask for those tests themselves. The APT works as a rehearsal of the format and an independent reference point, but admission depends on the schools on your list.

### Can the score change?

Yes, though not quickly. Peers improve too, so unless your child progresses faster than the year group the number stays much the same. That is why the sub-skill breakdown matters more than the overall figure.

### Does ALBION run the APT, and what does it cost?

The test runs on aptonline.co.uk, where, according to the site, a parent can register directly. How it relates to ALBION's assessment, whether it can be taken through us and what it costs, we explain at the first conversation, after the application form.

### What should we do with a low score?

Avoid conclusions from a single number. Find out which section pulled the score down and whether English is the cause, then set the result beside other evidence about the pupil. Lessons, a different entry point or a different school list come after that.
