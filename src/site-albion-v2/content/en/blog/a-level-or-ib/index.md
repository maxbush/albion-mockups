# A-Level or IB: Which Fits Your Target University

Neither qualification is stronger in itself. What matters is the offer the chosen course will make and what the school can teach. Cambridge, for one, publishes typical offers for both A-Levels and the IB, but expresses them differently: letters for A-Levels, points plus Higher Level grades for the IB. It is better to choose backwards, from the course, than forwards, from habit.

## How the two qualifications are built

| | A-Level | IB Diploma |
|---|---|---|
| Subjects | Usually three, sometimes four, over the last two years | Six from different groups: three or four at Higher Level (HL), the rest at Standard Level (SL) |
| Compulsory core | None | Extended Essay of up to 4,000 words, TOK, CAS |
| Grading | A grade per subject, from A* down | Up to 45 points: 42 for the subjects and up to 3 for the EE and TOK together |
| What an offer looks like | Letters, such as A*AA | A points total plus requirements for HL |
| What the student gets | Depth in a few subjects | Breadth and independent projects |

CAS earns no points, but the diploma is not awarded without it. The main exam session for both falls in May and June.

## What universities usually ask for

For A-Level, Oxford gives AAA as its minimum standard offer, and on many courses, above all in the sciences, it asks for at least one A*. In its published guidance Cambridge gives typical A-Level offers of A*AA or A*A*A, and typical IB offers of 40 to 42 points out of 45, with 776 or 777 at Higher Level. Some courses may also require a 7 in a particular subject.

These are the conditions the universities published themselves. Check the requirements for your course and your year of entry on the university's own site, because they change.

## When A-Levels fit

- The direction is already clear, and the course requires particular subjects, such as maths and physics.
- The student is strongest in three subjects and has little use for the rest.
- The schools on the shortlist teach A-Levels.

## When the IB fits

- The direction is still open, and several kinds of course need to stay possible.
- The student is comfortable combining humanities, maths and a language.
- They can sustain long independent projects and keep to a schedule across several pieces of work at once.

These are rules of thumb, not rules. The one firm conclusion is that the qualification must deliver the right subjects at the right level, with the grades the course asks for.

## What to weigh when choosing a school

Not every school teaches both qualifications, so the choice of school and the choice of qualification are linked. At 16+ entry to a sixth form, a school tests readiness for the particular subjects the student has chosen, as our page on [entrance exams](/private-schools/entrance-exams/) explains. When a child moves between systems, say from the IB to A-Levels, the gaps have to be closed quickly, which usually means tutors and an intensive course.

## A route in four steps

1. Settle the course and the universities, and write down the requirements: subjects, level, grades.
2. Find out which qualification the shortlisted schools teach.
3. Set the result against the student's real level. An [academic assessment](/assessment/) helps here, since it shows where a child stands against British peers.
4. Plan predicted grades early, because the terms of an offer depend on them.

## Questions and answers

### Which is easier, A-Levels or the IB?

There is no single answer. A-Levels go deeper in three subjects, the IB is broader and includes independent projects, and "easier" depends on the student. Choosing by difficulty is a poorer guide than choosing by the course's requirements.

### Can I get into Oxford or Cambridge with the IB?

Cambridge publishes typical IB offers: 40 to 42 points out of 45, with 776 or 777 at HL. For Oxford, check each course's requirements on the university's site.

### Can a student switch from the IB to A-Levels?

Such cases do occur. What matters is that the subjects the course requires are covered by the time the application goes in, and that the timetable leaves room to prepare.
