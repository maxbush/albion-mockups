# The Personal Statement for British Universities

The personal statement is the only place in the UCAS application where the candidate speaks in their own voice. It is also the part most applicants get wrong: they write an essay on "why I love the subject" instead of evidence of academic interest.

## What admissions tutors read

Not motivation — evidence:

- **what you have done with the subject** — reading beyond the syllabus, research, projects, olympiads;
- **how you think** — reflection on experience, not a list of achievements;
- **why this course** — knowledge of the specific programme, not of "a prestigious subject in general".

Roughly 80% of the text is academic substance, 20% context and personality. A frequent mistake of international applicants is translating the idea of a "motivation letter" literally: a British PS is built differently from a school essay or an American college essay.

## Requirements by discipline

- **Law** — analysis and argument; the LNAT essay connects logically with the PS.
- **Medicine** — work experience and reflection on it are mandatory; without clinical context a PS does not pass.
- **Engineering/mathematics** — problems and research experience instead of "a love of physics".
- **Humanities** — reading and critical engagement with sources.

## How we work on the application

A subject tutor and a consultant take the draft down to its substance: first what to say (profile and evidence), then how to say it (structure and language). The candidate always writes — committees recognise an adult hand, and at interview they test what is written.

## Questions

**One PS for all five choices?** There is only one — UCAS sends a single text to every university. That is why the list is built from neighbouring subjects.
**How many characters?** ~4,000 including spaces ⏳ or the new structured-answer format — confirmed per admission cycle.
**Do you "write it for me"?** No — we edit and direct. A borrowed text falls apart at interview.
