Private Schools in the UK: How to Choose and Get In

Three facts decide which independent schools are realistic for a child: age, current academic level and the country the family will be living in. Entry happens at fixed ages between 7+ and 16+, fees are charged by the term, and our own work begins with an academic assessment, well before a shortlist exists.

Entry points, age by age

Independent schools admit at fixed points: 7+ or 8+ into a prep school, 11+ or 13+ into a senior school, 16+ into the sixth form. Each point has its own tests and its own level of competition. A child who misses one is not necessarily out of the system, but the route changes.

Entry pointSchoolHow applicants are assessed
7+ / 8+Prep school, Years 3–4School's own assessments in English, maths and reasoning, plus an interview
11+Senior school, Year 7ISEB Pre-Test, CAT4, some schools' own papers, interview
13+Senior school, Year 9Common Entrance or school papers, interview
16+Sixth form, Year 12Subject tests for A-Level or IB, GCSE results, interview; UKiset often asked of overseas applicants

Wherever the child enters, the exams at the end are shared: GCSE at the close of Key Stage 4, then A-Level or the IB. What differs is how selective entry is and how strong the peer group is. UKiset, which some schools use in place of British grades, is a separate assessment for pupils educated outside the British system. Which test a school asks for is the school's decision and can change from year to year, so we check it when the shortlist is drawn up. Our page on 11+, 13+ and 16+ entrance exams goes through each one.

Day, boarding or something between

A day school assumes the family lives within reach of it, which for most of our families means London or the home counties. A boarding school suits a family based overseas, or a teenager who wants a full school community. Weekly boarding (home at weekends) and flexi boarding (individual nights) sit between the two.

Co-educational and single-sex schools both produce strong results, so that choice rests on the child's temperament and the family's preference. Location is the first filter for a day school. For boarding it matters less than the size of the school and the quality of its pastoral care.

What a term costs

Schools charge by the term, three terms a year. The ranges below are published market figures per term, not ALBION's own prices.

FormatTypical range per term
Boarding, pupils aged 8–13£9,500–15,000
Boarding, pupils aged 11–18£13,000–18,000
Day school£6,000–9,000

Each school sets its own fee.

ALBION's fees are separate and are quoted after the application form and a first conversation. They depend on the child's age, the length of the shortlist and the amount of preparation. Tutoring is billed at published hourly rates, from £65 an hour for academic subjects.

State schools and international families

State schools are funded by the government and are, as a rule, closed to families without UK status. Grammar schools are state schools too, selecting by an 11+ test, so the same rule applies. For most international families the question is therefore which independent school, not whether to go private.

When you may not need a consultant

If your family lives in the UK, your child is already in the British system and you want a local prep school with modest competition, much of the process can be done yourself. Schools publish their requirements. A consultant earns the fee when the child has studied abroad and not sat British tests, or when time is short. The same goes for a list that mixes schools with different selection formats, and for a profile that is not standard.

Where ALBION comes in

ALBION's work in British education dates from 2010. An admission begins with an academic assessment: ALBION teachers compare the child with British peers at independent schools, using past papers and a one-to-one lesson, and write a report for each subject. If the picture is awkward, we say so at this point, before any contract.

From the assessment our consultants draw up a list of up to ten schools, each with reasons, and recommend settling on five. The consultant then handles registration, letters and deadlines, while ALBION tutors prepare the child for tests and interviews.

We take no commission from schools, and nothing on a shortlist has been paid for. For pupils whose admission we handled, guardianship is available, but places are few.

Of ALBION's pupils, 96% receive a place at the school they chose. That is a record, not a promise, because each school makes its own decision. When the preferred school withdrew its place

A sixth-form applicant aged 18+ had little room for manoeuvre, since few schools take pupils at that age. She held offers from two of them. The school she preferred withdrew its place in July, after an inspection, when her visa application was already under way, and the placement had to be rebuilt. It is the reason we never plan an admission around a single offer. Other cases are collected on the cases page.

From practice: when a school says "he cannot cope"

Two brothers were at English boarding schools. The elder was bright, with strong emotional intelligence, but had learning differences and difficulty with recall. His school refused to move him to A-level because he had missed the minimum GCSE results in English and maths. The school had long known about his needs but said no outright by May. Without the required GCSE results, private schools usually do not take pupils into A-level, so a new school was needed urgently. We found a small independent school with small classes and very attentive teachers, and alongside A-level he resat GCSE maths and English. He liked it there, carried on, and now has a chance of university. If one school says "he cannot cope", it does not mean the child cannot: the task is to find the environment that suits him.

From practice: a refusal as the start of a conversation

The children of one family were at an international school in Europe and studied with our tutors. The family decided to move to another country for business, and we advised them remotely at every stage. The children differed in level and character, and placing them in one school seemed unrealistic, but we found a newly opened campus of a well-known British school. The other children were accepted, and the youngest son was refused: his entrance tests were weak. We advised the family not to treat the refusal as final but to meet the school and show a plan under which the boy would work with tutors all summer. The school reconsidered, and the children now study together.

Questions and answers

We have already moved to the UK. Can my child attend a state school?+

It depends on your status and your address. State schools in England generally allocate places by where the family lives, not by test results, so we need the postcode before we can say anything useful. We work through these cases individually, before the shortlist is drawn up.

What costs come on top of the termly fee?+

A registration fee and exam fees. Boarding adds travel and holidays, and a London day school adds housing and the daily commute. The school gives the full list and the final figure.

Does my child need UKiset?+

It depends on the school and the year. Some schools ask for it from pupils outside the British system, others do not, and we check this for each school on your list.

Are league tables a good guide?+

They mostly show how selective a school is, and selective schools admit strong pupils. Better questions are how competitive entry is, whether the school suits the child, and whether the child can sustain its pace.

Do we need a guardian?+

Yes, if the parents live abroad: a British school will ask for an official guardian for any child under 18. The guardian looks after health, welfare, holidays and daily matters. How ALBION approaches this is set out on our guardianship page.

When should we start?+

Our working figure is one to two years before the entry year, enough for assessment, tests, visits and applications. A later start is not the end of the road, but the shortlist is built differently.
