Private Schools in the UK: How to Choose and Get In

Educational guardianship for pupils at UK schools

A UK school will normally expect a pupil under 18 who has come from abroad to have a named guardian in the country. Guardianship for international students at UK schools covers the child's health, welfare and day-to-day decisions, and ALBION carries it out to the AEGIS standard. We take on few pupils and do not advertise the service.

Book a consultation

What an educational guardian in the UK does

In school guardianship in the UK the school entrusts the guardian with the child's health and wellbeing and with practical decisions: travel, accommodation, exeat weekends and holidays. For a boarding school guardian, exeat weekends and holidays are where the role is most visible, because the child is away from school. For the parents the guardian is their representative in the UK, acting for the child where they cannot be present.

A guardian does not stand in for the parents. The guardian is a responsible adult with the authority to act.

Who we can take on

Our UK guardianship services are limited to a few places. Guardianship, as we run it, is personal and needs a consultant who stays closely involved, so we offer it chiefly to pupils whose admission our team handled. Where we did not guide the placement, we are unlikely to be able to help.

There is no fee on this page. Terms are agreed with families whose boarding school admission or other school placement we are managing. Pupils at day schools can have academic and practical coordination if the family asks for it.

What a guardian does

The guardian is a consultant who has known the pupil since admission: academic and personal profile, goals, how the child has settled. A family therefore does not have to explain the background each time a new question arises.

The consultant attends meetings with the school, reviews academic progress and checks that agreements are kept. The consultant also keeps the school and the parents informed of each other, so that neither hears of a problem last, and explains the British system, the programmes and the school's internal procedures. Where a pupil is doing steadily well, the aim is to hold that level, adjust the subject load and plan the next steps. Where difficulties appear, whether academic, behavioural or practical, the consultant joins the conversations with the school and draws up a plan to keep the pupil on course.

Specialists are brought in when needed: educational psychologists, study skills coaches, subject tutors, specialists in assessing learning needs and medical consultants. The guardianship team is led by co-founder Altynay Yeshmatova, who is introduced on the team page.

Sometimes repeated signs suggest that a pupil may benefit from a specialist assessment. It is then raised carefully with the family and the school, and a support plan follows under professional supervision. Some situations are beyond what a guardian can resolve alone, and the family is told so at once.

From practice

A pupil with good grades. We placed a strong student at a demanding international sixth-form college and became his guardians while his parents were far away. He coped with the programme, but the intensity of study and some aspects of college life were hard for him, and with good grades that is difficult for a school or distant parents to notice. From regular conversations we saw recurring signs and tactfully suggested a professional assessment to his parents. It explained part of the difficulties and gave a basis for choosing support.

A girl with "difficult behaviour". She joined a boarding school at 13+ and her guardian was a member of our staff. Over a year to eighteen months she collected about 90 reprimands, and it reached a final warning and the threat of exclusion. Preparing for the hearing, we asked the school for all its records and saw the pattern: forgetfulness and unfinished tasks in lessons, but excellent concentration in one-to-one sessions with a tutor. We suspected the school had missed an attention difficulty and, with her parents, consulted an education lawyer. We presented the committee with a letter saying so, the school withdrew its accusations, and we secured an independent assessment, not by doctors linked to the school. The assessment confirmed the suspicion and she stayed at the school. The guardian was the child's voice: parents far away do not know the local system and could not have gone through this alone.

An accident at the weekend. An eight-year-old girl was in her first year at a boarding school, her parents lived abroad, and we were guardians to all four children in the family. At a weekend activity an instructor showed a skateboard trick, no mouthguards were issued, the girl fell and the board knocked out a permanent tooth. Treatment would last years, her mother's English was not enough for such a matter, and her father wanted to "sue everyone", while her mother wanted the girls to stay at their favourite school. We found in the school's policies its responsibility for incidents on its premises, coordinated the school, the hospital and the parents, met the head and prepared a firm but friendly letter on the parents' behalf. So that the family would not spend money on lawyers, we involved our tutor, a former barrister. The school agreed that insurance would cover all treatment up to the age of 18 and did not charge for that term. Both sisters still study there.

When not everything is in our power. A capable girl was heading for A-level, and we chose a strong school and found housing opposite it. In the first two days her guardian noticed clear difficulties with attention and routine: the girl could not organise her day and slept through lessons. She had chosen the sciences after a television series and had no real base, which showed in the first tests. With the school we chose other subjects, arranged tutors and a holiday intensive, and met her parents many times. Her parents decided to take her home, and we think that was the right decision. Not every story ends in victory: our task is to support, look for strategies and say honestly what works and what does not.

Questions and answers

Do I need a guardian to study in the UK?+

If you are under 18 and have come from abroad, normally yes: the school expects a named guardian in the country, and that includes a 16-year-old. The details depend on the school and are settled before term starts.

Can a relative act as guardian?+

The school decides whom it accepts in the role. It is best to ask before the place is confirmed, not afterwards.

Can we take guardianship without using ALBION for admissions?+

As a rule, no. The service is intended for pupils whose admission our team handled, and places are few. If your circumstances are unusual, we reply individually.

How does guardianship relate to choosing a school?+

It is worth considering while the shortlist is still being drawn. If a school has not yet been chosen, entrance exam preparation and the admissions steps can be arranged so that support carries on once term begins.
