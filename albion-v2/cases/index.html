Case Studies: How UK School and University Applications Turned Out

All the stories are anonymised: no names, no school or university names, and ages only where they do not identify the family. Seven school admission stories are told in full, the rest briefly, with a link to the page that tells each one in full. Where a caveat applies, it follows in italics, because a case shows what happened for one child, not what will happen for yours. Several did not go to plan. We have left them as they were.

Book a consultation

An offer is not yet a place

A young woman from abroad, aged 18, was applying for A-Levels, and few schools accept students over 18. ALBION prepared her for IELTS (the school took her further). She wrote the entrance tests well and held two offers. In July, with her visa in progress, the school she had chosen said that after an inspection it could not take her.

The team rebuilt the placement. A specialist assessment was needed, which meant persuading the student herself: at 18, it cannot go ahead without her consent. The team handled the paperwork and the correspondence with the school, and the assessment led to support in her exams. She was also entered for an additional A-Level in Russian, with a tutor.

She is now studying, with that support in place, and we are discussing help with her university applications.

Schools can withdraw a place even in July. The university stage of this story is still open.

The emergency exam and extra time from this story are described on the A-Level tutors page.

Two years to reach 13+

A boy in Year 6 at a British school had clear ability, but his English and academic groundwork both needed attention. His parents hoped for a place at one of the most selective boarding schools at 13+.

We suggested buying him time: two further years, Years 7 and 8, at a strong co-educational boarding prep school. The first year was demanding, with a new curriculum, language, culture and boarding life, and the family, the school and ALBION's guardians worked together. The family still wanted the most selective senior schools. ALBION spoke plainly about how competitive entry is and supported those applications, but they produced no offers. The team then reassessed what he needed, academically and pastorally, and applied to a different set of selective schools, a few of them after their normal deadlines.

Every one of them offered him a place. He is now at the school his family chose, doing well and happy there.

The first round of applications ended without a single offer, despite his evident ability.

This case is also mentioned in the parents' guide.

One unfamiliar name on the shortlist

A very able girl at an international school, with her family abroad, was moving to a British school in Year 10. Families overseas tend to know only the best-known names.

After assessing her level, ALBION built a shortlist of selective schools. Next to the familiar names it included an academically very strong school that few foreign families have heard of. The family visited every school on the list and liked this one best. She sat entrance exams at three.

Three offers came from three schools. She chose the one her family had not known about, and sat her GCSEs and A-Levels there and finished with excellent results.

The shortlist was built for this girl and this family, and it would not suit another child. We quote no exact grades.

This case is also mentioned on the private schools page.

The waiting list

A girl sat entrance exams for Year 9 at several highly selective schools. Two offers arrived, but the school her family had ranked first put her on its waiting list.

ALBION's advice was to accept one of the confirmed offers rather than build everything on a list. Over the following months the team kept in touch with the first-choice school, restating the family's interest and watching the list, and agreed with the parents beforehand what to do if a place appeared late.

About ten days before term, one did. The family said yes quickly, having already thought it through, and she has settled well, in lessons and with friends.

Nobody can predict a waiting list. The family took on a real risk: withdrawing late from the confirmed school could have cost a term's fees.

This case is also mentioned in the parents' guide and on the entrance exams page.

A GCSE resit with two weeks to spare

A pupil at a British boarding school planned to stay on for A-Levels, but their GCSE results ruled that out, and term began in less than two weeks.

With those grades, a place straight onto A-Levels was effectively closed. The team looked elsewhere and found a small, supportive school with a one-year GCSE resit programme, where each pupil gets real attention.

The grades improved markedly. The pupil moved on to A-Levels, did well in the exams and is now finishing university.

The route cost a year. With those results, there was no quicker one.

This case is also mentioned on the boarding schools and GCSE tutors pages.

A sporting profile, and a shortlist that worked

A boy applying to boarding schools at 13+ had a strong sporting profile. We drew up his boarding shortlist. Once he was at school, we suggested taking up a team sport, and he joined a school team.

Every school on the list made him an offer, and the family chose one. He later captained the team.

Not every shortlist ends like this, as the waiting list and the 13+ case above show.

This case is also mentioned on the boarding schools page.

Year 9 instead of Year 10

A family living abroad hoped to move their son, then at an international school, into Year 10 of a British boarding school. The normal application window had closed. He was still building his English and some subjects had gaps, although our academic assessment showed real potential.

We advised against rushed applications. For nearly a year he worked with ALBION tutors in English, mathematics and the sciences, and built the habits a British curriculum expects. Applications then went to several academically selective schools outside the usual cycle. One declined, another offered Year 9 in place of Year 10, and the family's preferred school also declined Year 10. ALBION went back to that school and proposed Year 9 as a better starting point for a boy still finding his feet in a new language and school system.

The school agreed and offered a place, which gave him an extra year to build a sound foundation.

He entered a year lower than the family first intended.

This case is also mentioned on the entrance exams page.

More stories, by topic

These stories are told briefly, and the full version is on the page the heading links to.

Schools

When a school says "he cannot cope". A school refused to move the elder of two brothers to A-Level because of his GCSE results. We quickly found a small school with small classes, he resat GCSE maths and English and carried on. The environment had to change, rather than the place at his old school being fought for.

A refusal as the start of a conversation. The youngest child was refused a place at a campus of a British school abroad: his entrance tests were weak. His parents came to a meeting with a plan of summer lessons, and the school reconsidered. It does not always go this way.

Universities and Oxbridge

Oxford at any price. A pupil was heading for Oxford to read law without being able to say why, changed subject twice and settled on history. He reached the interview and did not get an Oxford offer, but four Russell Group offers came. Oxford did not make an offer, and we say so plainly.

Five offers and a turn in August. The applicant's school would not give predicted grades or a reference, so we submitted him as an independent applicant. Five offers came, and in August his firm choice confirmed him after he had settled on his insurance, so the matter was settled through Clearing. Neither we nor his school had met this situation before.

IB closed overnight. The IB programme in the student's country was closed overnight, and on the UCAS deadline day her application had to be rebuilt within an hour. Three offers came, two from Russell Group universities. It also helped that the family already held a two-year British visa.

A transfer to a university near Wall Street. A student at a Californian university who wanted a career in finance transferred to a city college in New York close to Wall Street. Most of the work was months of waiting for answers and documents.

A conditional offer with no fallback. The US has no insurance offer as UCAS does, so the maths condition became a risk. We prepared a plan B in advance, and the condition was met.

Weak grades are not the end of the road. A graduate with weak grades entered a US state university and later transferred to a strong hospitality university with an honest essay. Study took a year longer.

MBA and executive study

Two profiles, one story. The head of a fashion brand who was also a non-executive director of a large company was admitted to the one-year Executive Diploma at Saïd when her two profiles were built into one line.

The person's story, not the company's. An entrepreneur's first drafts were entirely about his business. The application worked once it became the story of the man himself, and he was admitted at the first attempt.

All three risks in one application. A candidate with a strong profile had references written for another purpose and too many achievements. The application was simplified, and he was admitted.

Guardianship

A pupil with good grades. From regular conversations his guardian noticed that a sixth-form college student was struggling despite good grades, and tactfully suggested an assessment to his parents.

A girl with "difficult behaviour". About 90 reprimands in a year and a half and the threat of exclusion. In the school's records we saw attention difficulties it had missed and secured an independent assessment. She stayed at the school.

An accident at the weekend. An eight-year-old girl lost a permanent tooth at a weekend activity. We coordinated the school, the hospital and the parents, the school's insurance covered treatment up to 18, and the relationship survived.

When not everything is in our power. A capable girl could not settle into a routine or build on her chosen subjects, her parents decided to take her home, and we think that was the right decision. Not every story ends in victory.

The figures behind the stories

ALBION's work in British education dates from 2010. Over that time 96% of pupils have been placed at their chosen school, and every client (100%) has received at least one Russell Group offer. Three in four of the pupils we support have reached interview at Oxford or Cambridge. These describe past work and do not forecast yours: schools and universities make the decisions.
