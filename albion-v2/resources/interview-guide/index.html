Guide to the Admissions Interview

An interview is the part of admissions where the paperwork stops and a person has to speak. At school, a child should be able to speak naturally about their interests. At Oxford or Cambridge, the candidate must reason through the subject and discuss what is in the application, not recite it. The two conversations are quite different, and so is the preparation.

Interview procedures vary from school to school and change between admissions cycles. Check each school's or college's own guidance for your year.

Two conversations, side by side

School interviewOxford or Cambridge interview
Who is invitedApplicants at many schools, from prep school to sixth formCandidates whose written application and, where required, test result have been read and short-listed
What it is aboutThe child: how they talk, what they care about, why they want this schoolThe subject: how the candidate reasons and how deep their knowledge goes
Who preparesThe family, with a tutor or consultant if wantedThe candidate with a mentor, and a subject tutor for gaps

What a school interviewer is looking for

A school wants to know who is coming through its door. The conversation usually touches on how the child communicates, what they are interested in outside lessons, and why they want to be at this particular school. With a younger child the emphasis falls on conversation more than on content, while a sixth-form applicant can expect questions about the subjects they intend to study.

The difficult part is not the questions but the nerves. A child who has never been interviewed tends to answer in one word or to recite what they think is wanted. The most useful preparation is a few practice conversations in which the child learns that a full sentence and an honest opinion are enough. At 16+, subject questions follow from the A-Level choices, so a short conversation about why those subjects is worth rehearsing.

For boarding, many schools also like to understand how a child feels about living away from home. We cannot say how any given school handles this, so ask the school.

ALBION runs mock interviews before the real ones, on subjects and on the child, and gives feedback afterwards. Each school sets its own timetable, so the mocks are placed to match.

The Oxford and Cambridge interview

Getting invited is the first result, and not a small one. Of ALBION's pupils, three in four are invited to interview at Oxford or Cambridge, and that figure describes our work with particular profiles and does not predict the next family's outcome. Oxford interviews take place in December in the current cycle, so the preparation has to be well under way by the autumn. Check the date for your own year.

What the interview is built around is the subject. The college wants to see academic argument and depth of knowledge, not general confidence. It follows that the interview rewards a candidate who has actually read and thought about the subject, and punishes one who has learnt to sound fluent.

Preparation at ALBION takes the form of mock sessions with a review after each, and what the sessions reveal goes back into the plan. Gaps in the subject itself are closed by a subject tutor. Interview preparation with sessions and review is part of the Oxbridge programme, while tutor hours are paid separately: specialist tutors start from £110 an hour for interview and test preparation and senior specialists from £150. The Oxbridge admissions page explains the whole programme, and the test guide covers the written side that comes before it.

No one can promise an offer. A college decides, and strong candidates compete for each place.

Saying aloud what you have written

Everything in a personal statement is a possible question. If it mentions a book, the candidate should be able to say what the argument was, what they thought of it and where they disagreed. If it mentions an interest, they should be able to explain why it matters to them beyond the sentence on the page. A candidate who cannot explain why they chose the subject is in difficulty, because that is the first thing a thoughtful interviewer asks.

A useful exercise is to read the statement and, for each claim, say aloud what an interviewer might ask next. Where the answer is thin, the claim is not ready, and it is better to find that in practice than in the room. Our page on the personal statement explains how to write one with this in mind, and the UCAS support page covers the application it belongs to.

Writing well and speaking well are separate skills. A candidate can be clear on paper and lose confidence in an unfamiliar setting, and that kind of confidence is built over a long time and not in a fortnight. It is a good reason to practise early.

Preparing in the weeks before

Start by speaking. Answer questions aloud, in full sentences, with someone who will interrupt and ask why. Reading answers silently teaches little about how they will sound.

Next, let thinking be audible. In a subject interview a considered, partly right answer that shows the route taken is often worth more than a quick answer that shows nothing. A candidate who goes quiet for a moment is not failing, provided they say when they are thinking.

Third, add depth where it is needed. If mock sessions show a weak area in the subject, that is a job for study, not for rehearsal. Finally, keep the preparation proportionate. Some things take years to build and cannot be changed in two months, and a candidate who is rested and honest does better than one who has memorised.

Habits that weaken an interview

Memorised answers: they sound rehearsed and fall apart at the first follow-up question.

Long monologues: a short answer that invites the next question is better than a lecture.

Bluffing: an interviewer who knows the subject will find the edge of what you know, and an honest "I am not sure, but I would guess" serves better than a confident error.

Panic at silence: a pause to think is normal, and it is better to say so than to fill the gap with anything.

Preparing only the question "Why this subject?": it will be asked, but follow-ups are about the substance behind the answer.

Arriving with nothing to ask: for school interviews especially, one or two real questions about the school show genuine interest.

Questions and answers

What do school interviewers actually ask?+

Usually questions about the child: interests, favourite subjects, why this school, and how they handle things that go wrong. Sixth-form applicants may be asked about the subjects they intend to study. The form varies by school, so we advise asking the school what to expect.

How many Oxbridge applicants are invited to interview?+

That varies by course and by year, and the universities publish their own figures, which are worth reading for your subject. For ALBION's own pupils, three in four are invited, which says something about our work with particular profiles and does not predict anyone's result.

Can I prepare for an Oxford or Cambridge interview on my own?+

Many applicants do. Practice with a teacher or a mentor who will push back is worth much more than rehearsing alone. A tutor or consultant helps most where there are gaps in the subject or the candidate struggles to speak with confidence.

Do I need to memorise my personal statement?+

No. You need to understand every sentence in it well enough to discuss it. A memorised statement sounds flat, and an understood one lets you follow the interviewer's questions.

What if I do not get an interview invitation?+

At that point the application also includes other universities, prepared under a backup strategy, so the next step is to weigh the options you have. Not being invited is a decision of the colleges and says little about you as a student.

Is the interview more important than grades and tests?+

It is one part of the decision, read alongside grades, the personal statement and the test result where one is required. A good interview does not replace weak grades, and strong grades do not settle the interview.
