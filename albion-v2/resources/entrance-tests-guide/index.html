Guide to Entrance Tests for UK Schools and Universities

Parents and applicants often ask for a list of the tests their child or they must sit. A list would be easy to write and would soon be wrong, because the answer depends on three things: the school or course, the year of entry, and where the candidate is studying now. This guide explains what the main tests are for and how to work out which ones apply. It covers school tests first, then the university tests that are tied to particular courses, and ends with preparation and the limits of what a test can tell anyone.

Book a consultation

Requirements change, and test names and rules do so from cycle to cycle. Treat this page as a map of the ground, and check the school's or university's own requirements for your year before you register for anything.

School tests: what each one measures

For admission to an independent school, no single exam applies everywhere. Schools pick from a small group of tests, add their own papers and, in most cases, an interview. The table describes the group by what each test is trying to find out.

TestWhat it looks atCan you prepare for it?
ISEB Pre-TestEnglish, maths, verbal and non-verbal reasoning, online and adaptive, with the score standardised for ageYes, up to a point: ISEB publishes material for families, and practice mostly removes surprise and builds pace
CAT4Verbal, non-verbal, quantitative and spatial reasoningNot like a syllabus, but the format can be met in advance
CEM Select, QuestReasoning and academic skills, requested by individual schoolsThe school says which applies
Common Entrance, set by ISEBSubjects that the school specifies, at 11+ or 13+Yes, from the school's own list of subjects
UKisetAcademic potential and English, set against British pupils of the same ageMostly through English and familiarity with the online format
The school's own papersWhatever the school wants to see, at any ageFrom past papers, if the school provides them

Two things in this table are worth a closer look.

The Pre-Test is taken in Year 6 or 7. At 11+ and in some schools at 13+, it acts as a first filter that decides whom the school invites further, so it comes well before Common Entrance for a 13+ candidate. A parent registers the child through the ISEB portal, and each school sets its own thresholds and timetable.

UKiset was developed with Cambridge University Press & Assessment for pupils educated outside the British system. Most candidates sit it online under remote invigilation. Schools tend to read the result as a profile before the rest of the file and not as a pass mark. One set of results can go to several schools in the UKiset network, which is a growing group and not every school, so it is worth checking the shortlist before anyone registers.

Working out what your child will sit

Four questions settle it, in this order.

  1. Which schools are on the list? The tests follow from the schools, so choose the schools first.
  2. What is the entry point? A seven-year-old, a thirteen-year-old and a sixteen-year-old are tested differently. The detail by age is on our page about 11+, 13+ and 16+ entrance exams.
  3. Where does the child study now? A child at a British prep school generally follows the standard route. One at a local school abroad may need UKiset or a school's own online tasks, with English checked first. A child at an international school on a British curriculum is compared with British independent schools before tests are chosen.
  4. Does each school actually accept what you plan to send? Ask. Whether a school takes UKiset, offers its own papers instead of Common Entrance, or allows a retake is the school's decision.

University tests

At university level the logic is different. Tests are tied to courses, not to the university as a whole, and for some courses the result helps decide who is invited to interview. Most courses in the UK do not require one.

LNAT is used for law at Oxford and a number of other universities, and has two parts: verbal reasoning and an argumentative essay. TARA and the TSA, which have changed names and course coverage in recent years, are the thinking and reasoning tests at Oxford and Cambridge. TMUA serves mathematics, economics and computer science at some universities, and MAT is Oxford's mathematics test. UCAT is for medicine and dentistry; the older BMAT has been discontinued. For US undergraduate admission there is the SAT, and for business schools and master's programmes the GMAT or GRE, many schools accepting either.

International applicants are also usually asked to show their English, often through IELTS, with the score set by each course.

Which test your course needs in your year is confirmed with the college or faculty. Registration often closes before the application itself does, which is why the plan is built backwards from test dates. Course-by-course detail is on our page about university admissions tests, and the Oxford and Cambridge cycle is covered under Oxbridge admissions.

How to prepare

Start with a diagnostic. The candidate does past papers under time, and the tutor looks at where marks go: pace, one type of question, or the wording. Without that, practice tends to repeat what the candidate already does well.

Then work on the specific test. LNAT calls for one technique on the verbal section and another on the essay, so they are practised separately. TMUA and MAT rest on the A-Level syllabus they grow out of, so a mathematics tutor builds from that. For the school tests, practice means getting used to the format, the adaptive logic and the clock.

For international candidates, English comes first. Language gaps distort every score, so it makes sense to deal with them before reasoning tests, not after. Our language preparation covers this.

Many candidates prepare alone, particularly for the ISEB Pre-Test, where there is official material. A tutor earns the fee when progress has stalled, time is short, or it is hard to see where marks are going. For entrance and interview preparation, specialist tutors at ALBION start from £110 an hour and senior specialists from £150; the prices page has the full scale. The number of lessons is set after the diagnostic.

What a test does not tell you

A test records one performance on one day. It says nothing about curiosity, persistence or how a child will cope away from home. It is also one input among several: schools read it with reports, references and an interview, and universities read it with grades, the personal statement and, for some courses, an interview. A weak result is not the end of an application, though it may change the list.

Nobody can promise a score. ALBION does not set marks or influence a decision, and the exam boards and schools run the tests. For the other half of selection, our interview guide covers what happens when the paper is over.

Questions and answers

Does every UK school require an entrance test?+

No. Many independent schools use tests, but which ones, and whether they are used at all, is each school's decision. The state sector selects differently and, as a rule, is closed to families without UK status.

We live abroad. Does my child need UKiset?+

Only if the schools on your list ask for it. Some accept it as a profile, others use their own online tasks. We check each school before registration, because the result can go to several schools and save a repeat sitting.

Can my child retake a test?+

It depends on the test and the school. Retake rules are set by exam boards and schools, so ask before you register.

Is the ISEB Pre-Test something a child can revise for?+

Not as a syllabus. It is adaptive and standardised for age, so what helps is familiarity with the format, pace and the question types. Official practice material exists, and a tutor can help where a particular weakness shows.

My child's English is not yet strong. Should we wait?+

First find out how far behind it is. If language is the main gap, the plan starts with English lessons, and the school list and entry point are chosen with the time available in mind.

How early do university tests need to be planned?+

Well before the application, because registration for a test often closes earlier than the application deadline. The date depends on the test and the candidate's starting level, which a first diagnostic shows.
